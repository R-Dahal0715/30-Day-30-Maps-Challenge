# Day 07 — Spatial Selection & Basic Spatial Analysis

## Overview

Day 7 marked the transition from basic GIS mapping and cartographic visualization toward **spatial analysis**.

The objective was to use existing Nepal spatial datasets from Phase 1 to investigate the spatial relationship between selected provinces, cities, and rivers. Rather than downloading new data, the existing Nepal provinces, cities, and river datasets were reused to practice reproducible spatial-selection workflows.

The analysis focused on **Gandaki and Koshi provinces**.

---

## Objective

The main objective was to learn how to:

* Select geographic features using attribute information.
* Select features based on their spatial relationship with other features.
* Distinguish between `within` and `intersect` spatial relationships.
* Export selected features as new analytical datasets.
* Preserve original datasets while creating derived GIS outputs.
* Improve map symbology so that analytical results can be interpreted clearly.

The central spatial question was:

> **Which cities and rivers are spatially associated with Gandaki and Koshi provinces?**

---

## Data Used

The analysis reused datasets developed during the earlier phases of the 30 Days GIS Challenge.

### 1. Nepal Provinces

**Geometry:** Polygon

Used to identify and select Gandaki and Koshi provinces.

### 2. Nepal Cities

**Geometry:** Point

Used to identify cities located within the selected provinces.

### 3. Nepal Rivers / Streams

**Geometry:** Line

Used to identify river and stream features intersecting the selected provinces.

No new external datasets were downloaded for Day 7.

---

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Project preparation

A new QGIS project was created for Day 7.

The existing Nepal province, city, and river layers were added to the project.

The CRS of the river dataset was also checked and corrected through proper reprojection to ensure that the layer was appropriately aligned with the other datasets.

---

### Step 2 — Selecting Gandaki and Koshi

The Nepal province layer was opened in the attribute table.

Gandaki and Koshi were selected using their attribute information.

This demonstrated **attribute-based selection**, where features are selected according to information stored in their attribute table.

Conceptually:

```text
Province attributes
        ↓
Gandaki OR Koshi
        ↓
Selected province polygons
```

---

### Step 3 — Selecting cities by location

The selected provinces were then used as the reference features for a spatial selection.

The city layer was selected using the spatial relationship:

> **within**

This identified city points located within Gandaki and Koshi.

The selected city features were then exported as a new analytical layer:

```text
Gandaki_Koshi_Cities
```

---

### Step 4 — Selecting rivers by location

The same approach was applied to the river dataset.

The spatial relationship used was:

> **intersect**

This identified river and stream features that intersected the selected Gandaki and Koshi province boundaries.

The selected features were exported as:

```text
Gandaki_Koshi_Rivers
```

---

## Important GIS Concepts Learned

### Attribute Selection vs Spatial Selection

Day 7 demonstrated the difference between two fundamental GIS operations.

**Attribute selection:**

```text
Province = Gandaki OR Koshi
```

uses information stored in the attribute table.

**Spatial selection:**

```text
Cities → within → Gandaki + Koshi
```

uses the geometric relationship between layers.

This distinction is fundamental to ecological and environmental GIS analysis.

---

### `Within` vs `Intersect`

The city analysis used:

```text
Cities
   ↓
within
   ↓
Selected provinces
```

while the river analysis used:

```text
Rivers
   ↓
intersect
   ↓
Selected provinces
```

These relationships answer different spatial questions and are important when designing GIS workflows for ecological research.

---

## A Funny but Useful Learning Moment 😂

There was a small moment of confusion during the river-selection analysis.

Initially, it appeared that the spatial selection had failed because the selected rivers were not visually obvious on the map.

After checking the workflow, it turned out that **the analysis was completely correct**.

The problem was simply that the selected rivers and the selected province areas were displayed using similar colors. The selected river segments therefore blended visually into the selected province symbology.

In other words:

> **The GIS analysis was right; my symbology was misleading me.** 😂

This was an important reminder that GIS has two separate but connected components:

1. **Analysis**
2. **Visualization**

A correct spatial operation can still be difficult to interpret if the cartographic styling does not provide sufficient visual contrast.

This experience reinforced the importance of checking the actual selection count and attribute table instead of judging an analytical result solely from its appearance on the map.

## Reflection

Day 7 represented an important shift in the learning process.

Earlier days focused primarily on:

> **How can I display geographic data effectively?**

Day 7 introduced a more analytical question:

> **How can I use geographic relationships between datasets to answer a spatial question?**

This is a fundamental transition from **map-making toward spatial analysis**.

The workflow also demonstrated an important principle that will become increasingly relevant in ecological GIS:

> **GIS is not just about producing maps. It is about using spatial relationships to extract information from geographic data.**

The same concepts can later be applied to questions such as:

* Which species observations occur inside protected areas?
* Which sampling sites are near rivers?
* Which roads intersect wildlife habitat?
* Which land-cover types occur within a study area?
* Which ecological observations fall inside a particular watershed?

Day 7 therefore established the foundation for more advanced spatial analysis in the later stages of the 30 Days GIS Challenge.
